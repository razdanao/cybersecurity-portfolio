# Case Study: Kevin Mitnick (Threat Actor Analysis)

| Question | Analysis |
|---|---|
| **Who** | Kevin Mitnick, once one of the most wanted computer criminals in the US |
| **Hacker type** | Started as a **black hat**, later became a **white hat**: a paid penetration tester, speaker and author |
| **Motive** | Largely intellectual challenge and curiosity rather than financial gain; he described the thrill of getting past defenses |
| **Internal or external?** | **External.** He had no authorized access to the networks he entered |

## What he did
- Broke into the networks of major telecom and technology companies and copied proprietary software.
- Intercepted and used stolen passwords, read private email and altered network configurations.
- Used cloned cellular phones to hide his location, an example of **spoofing** and telecom fraud.
- Relied heavily on **social engineering**: tricking employees into handing over passwords and access codes. He later wrote about this in *The Art of Deception* (2002).

## Controls that would have helped
| Control type | Example |
|---|---|
| **Administrative** | Security-awareness training and a rule never to share credentials or reset them for unverified callers |
| **Technical** | Hardened gateways and firewalls, strong authentication, monitoring for unusual access |
| **Physical** | Locks and access control on equipment rooms and premises |

The lesson: his main weapon was people, not code, so the strongest defense is training plus verification procedures.

## Outcome and takeaways
His case tested how courts handled computer crime and raised public awareness of networked-computer security. After his sentence he worked as a security consultant, trained organizations in social engineering defense, and shows how ethical hackers can turn offensive skill into defensive value.
